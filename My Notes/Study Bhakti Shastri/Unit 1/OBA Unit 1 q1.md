The four paths—karma-kāṇḍa, karma-yoga, jñāna-yoga, and dhyāna-yoga—are different stages of yoga, which means linking with the Supreme. These paths progress toward bhakti-yoga, as emphasized in Bhagavad-gītā 6.46.
Karma-kāṇḍa refers to Vedic injunctions promoting regulated sense gratification for material prosperity, elevation to higher planets, and enjoyment through wealth, beauty, and education.
Karma-yoga is performing prescribed duties without attachment.

Karma-kāṇḍa - Regulated Sense Gratification  

It attracts ignorant people with a poor fund of knowledge due to their strong attachment to sense gratification. It promises material enjoyment in heaven, including wine, women, and great opulence, which appeals to less intelligent individuals. Karma-kāṇḍīs mistakenly believe that the Vedas exist solely for material prosperity (BG 2.42-45, purports).  

  

Karma-yoga / Buddhi-yoga - Action According to Prescribed Duties  

Karma-yoga means performing one's prescribed duties with detachment. It is also known as niṣkāma-karma-yoga, where one performs duties without attachment to results and offers them to the Supreme Lord.  

Kṛṣṇa explains this principle to Arjuna when he asks whether renunciation or action is superior (BG 3.1-2). Kṛṣṇa clarifies that niṣkāma-karma-yoga is superior to karma-kāṇḍa, using the example of King Janaka, who performed his duties while remaining spiritually advanced (BG 3.20). Kṛṣṇa advises Arjuna to engage in work to set an example for common men and warns against premature renunciation.  
    
BG 3.9 clearly states that one should perform actions as a sacrifice (yajñārthāt karma), which frees one from bondage. The term mukta-saṅgaḥ is especially significant, as it refers to becoming free from the cycle of birth and death and detachment from the fruits of work.
One important point mentioned in chapter 5 is Work in Devotion is Superior to Renunciation, actually renunciation adn working in the spirit of renunciation are same.  One who performs Karma yoga is never entangled(BG5.7)
BG5.10 explains how one is unaffected by sin when doing karma-yoga

So there are philosophers, they are discriminating that, "This is matter . . ." Neti neti, "This is matter, and this is spirit."- (Srila Prabhupada Lecture BG 02.48-49, New York, April 1, 1966))"""

The first point to understand is that jñāna is received through disciplic succession, as it was originally understood by saintly kings. However, this succession was broken over time, leading to the loss of true knowledge (BG 4.2).
    
Kṛṣṇa explains that understanding karma is difficult, but through jñāna, one can burn the reactions of karma (BG 4.19). From BG 4.25-33, Kṛṣṇa describes different types of sacrifices, explaining that by performing them, one can attain jñāna, which burns all sinful reactions (BG 4.30).
    
The most essential point is that jñāna is attained by surrendering to a bona fide spiritual master and rendering service to him (BG 4.34). One who possesses true knowledge is never bewildered and sees all living entities as parts of Kṛṣṇa. Even the most sinful person can be delivered by the boat of knowledge (jñāna-plavenaiva, BG 4.36).
    
Over time, one attains the purest transcendental knowledge, and a person perfected in devotional service experiences this knowledge within himself (BG 4.38).
Ignorance is the cause of bondage, while knowledge leads to liberation (BG 4.38, Purport). One who has faith in the scriptures and follows the proper path attains jñāna, whereas an ignorant, faithless person who doubts the scriptures can never attain knowledge and remains unhappy in this life and the next (BG 4.39-40).**

Dhyāna-yoga: Meditative Absorption

When karma-yoga increases in knowledge and renunciation, the stage is called jñāna-yoga. When jñāna-yoga increases in meditation on the Supersoul by different physical processes, and the mind is on Him, it is called aṣṭāṅga-yoga. And, when one surpasses the aṣṭāṅga-yoga and comes to the point of the Supreme Personality of Godhead Kṛṣṇa, it is called bhakti-yoga, the culmination.(Purport BG6.47)

Dhyāna-yoga refers to fixing the mind on the Supreme through systematic control of the body, senses, and thoughts.
It consists of eight parts
	Yama (Don'ts – regulations and prohibitions)
	Niyama (Do's – rules and prescriptions)
	Āsana (bodily exercises)
	Prāṇāyāma (breathing exercises)
	Pratyāhāra (withdrawal of the senses from sense objects)
	Dhāraṇā (fixing the mind on the Lord)
	Dhyāna (intensified meditation on Kṛṣṇa)
	Samādhi (complete absorption/trance)

A true sannyāsī (renunciant) and yogī is one who performs duty without attachment to results, rather than someone who merely renounces work externally (BG 6.1).
Two stages of progress:
	Yogarurukṣu (beginner stage): One practices niṣkāma-karma-yoga, cultivates detachment, and follows Aṣṭāṅga-yoga, which gradually purifies the heart.
	Yogarūḍha (advanced stage): One is completely purified of material desires, neither acting for sense gratification nor engaging in fruitive activities.
Controlling the mind is essential in this process. Kṛṣṇa explains that it can be achieved through practice and detachment (BG 6.35-36).
Conclusion
The first six chapters of the Bhagavad-gītā systematically explain different paths of yoga, culminating in bhakti-yoga, which is declared the highest in BG 6.47. Karma-kāṇḍa entangles one in material bondage through fruitive activities, whereas karma-yoga purifies the heart by engaging in duty without attachment to results. As one progresses, jñāna-yoga brings deeper realization by distinguishing matter from spirit, while dhyāna-yoga refines the mind through aṣṭāṅga-yoga. However, in this age, aṣṭāṅga-yoga is impractical, though its principles, such as practice and detachment, can be effectively applied in bhakti-yoga. Ultimately, one must transcend material life and attain the stage of loving devotional service to Kṛṣṇa. By the grace of Srila Prabhupada, we are incredibly fortunate to understand this intricate system of yoga.

Introduction
Śrīmad Bhagavad-gītā is a divine conversation between the Supreme Personality of Godhead, Kṛṣṇa, and Arjuna on the battlefield of Kurukṣetra. It provides adetailed understanding of yoga, ultimately leading to bhakti-yoga, the highest goal. As stated in BG 1.1 (Purport): "It is the perfect theistic science spoken by the Supreme Personality of Godhead."

The Bhagavad-gītā describes different yoga paths, all culminating in bhakti-yoga:
- Karma-yoga (selfless action) – BG 3.9: "Work as a sacrifice for Lord."
- Jñāna-yoga (path of knowledge) – BG 4.10: "Understanding Kṛṣṇa leads to liberation."
- Dhyāna-yoga (meditation) – BG 6.33: "Astanga yoga is very hard as ."
- Bhakti-yoga (devotional service) – BG 6.47: "Kṛṣṇa declares the bhakti-yogī as the highest."

Bhakti-yoga is superior to all other paths, as Kṛṣṇa explains to Arjuna. Other yoga systems are impractical in Kali-yuga, whereas bhakti-yoga includes the essential elements of all other yogas. Furthermore, in Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam, Canto 11 (Uddhava Gītā), Kṛṣṇa confirms that bhakti is the highest form of worship.
